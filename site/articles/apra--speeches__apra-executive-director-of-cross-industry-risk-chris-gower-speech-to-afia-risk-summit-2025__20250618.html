APRA Executive Director of Cross-industry Risk Chris Gower speech to AFIA Risk Summit 2025

APRA Executive Director Chris Gower emphasised the growing operational resilience challenges facing Australia’s financial system amid complex technological, geopolitical, and third-party risks. His address to the AFIA Risk Summit on 18 June 2025 underscores the agency’s approach to ensuring financial stability while enabling responsible innovation.

Gower opened by drawing an analogy between the 1991 Perfect Storm, which was created by three converging meteorological events, and the current convergence of risks confronting financial institutions. He identified three primary risks driving operational vulnerability: pervasive technology dependence, increasing reliance on third-party providers, often offshore, and a shifting geopolitical landscape that intensifies cyber and operational threats. These overlapping pressures threaten the continuity of critical financial services and could undermine public trust and financial soundness if poorly managed.

He stressed that operational risks can escalate swiftly, damaging confidence and, ultimately, a firm’s viability. Well-prepared institutions invest proactively in resilience and develop robust incident response capabilities. At the system level, Gower highlighted the importance of collective preparedness to withstand geopolitical developments that may trigger significant operational disruptions. APRA aims to apply a balanced regulatory framework that supports prudent risk-taking and technological innovation without imposing undue constraints.

Gower referenced recent intelligence assessments that describe Australia’s threat environment as “multifaceted, merging, intersecting, concurrent and cascading,” with espionage and foreign interference risks at “extreme levels.” The National Defence Strategy has noted malign actors improving cyber capabilities, raising the risk of disruptions to critical infrastructure. Although these developments may seem remote from daily operations, they have practical consequences. Risks can manifest through traditional channels such as credit or market shocks or via less conventional means including sanctions enforcement or insider threats.

Gower illustrated these risks with concrete examples. Early in 2025, volatility spurred by shifts in international trade prompted APRA and other regulators to engage financial entities closely, assessing exposure and supporting longer-term planning. Superannuation funds faced coordinated cyber attacks that led to theft of some members’ retirement savings, highlighting the sophistication and motivation of threat actors. He also recalled how sanctions on Russia following the invasion of Ukraine forced Australian entities to tighten controls around prohibited transactions. Although limited in local impact, this episode demonstrates the operational and compliance complexities that transnational political events can trigger.

These instances, alongside lessons from the Silicon Valley Bank crisis and disruptions caused by service outages like Crowdstrike’s, underline the integration and interdependency inherent in the modern financial ecosystem. Global regulators are responding by working with industry to set minimum standards that enhance operational resilience, including securing premises, systems, and critical data and improving contingency strategies.

In Australia, collaboration between APRA, the Council of Financial Regulators, and industry focuses on three priorities. First, the imminent introduction of the prudential standard CPS 230 will drive enhanced supply chain risk awareness and continuity planning. Banks, insurers, and superannuation funds must comprehensively understand their critical third-party dependencies, routinely assess risks, forge strong supplier partnerships and monitor ongoing service integrity. CPS 230 builds on prior standards CPS 220, 231, 232, and 234, reflecting a continuous improvement philosophy in APRA’s prudential framework.

Second, cyber preparedness remains a top priority. Gower noted that many regulated entities have not yet met baseline cyber resilience requirements relative to evolving threats. APRA’s recent letter to superannuation funds reinforced the need for robust authentication, including accelerated deployment of multi-factor authentication for high-risk functions. As threats grow more severe and sophisticated, financial institutions must fortify defences to “batten down the hatches.”

Third, at the system level, APRA and its partners have launched work on the Council of Financial Regulators’ geopolitical program to better understand and mitigate emerging risks amid escalating geopolitical tensions.

For business leaders, Gower advises adopting a resilience mindset rather than focusing solely on compliance. Entities prepared under CPS 230 have demonstrated stronger risk frameworks and more effective operational response during material events. Critical questions remain straightforward despite technological complexity: are the right people allowed access to critical systems and information? Continuous reevaluation of these fundamentals must accompany innovations like artificial intelligence, which offer productivity gains but introduce new vulnerabilities.

Gower emphasised the importance of organisational risk culture, fostering communication, collaboration across teams, and encouraging employees to raise concerns. Scenario planning should factor in geopolitical shocks, cyber incidents and natural disasters, enabling identification of weaknesses and rapid remedial action. Given the inevitability of operational disruptions, tested incident response plans and clear communication channels with stakeholders are vital for coordinated crisis management.

APRA applies a risk-based, proportionate regulatory approach, differentiating between significant financial institutions (SFIs) and smaller entities. This calibration allows tailored risk management that accounts for operational complexity without burdening less complex players unnecessarily. For example, some provisions of CPS 230 have been deferred for non-SFIs until 1 July 2026, reflecting APRA’s commitment to balance safety with efficiency and innovation.

Similarly, APRA’s principles-based approach to emergent technologies seeks to harness benefits while remaining vigilant to risks, avoiding premature or overly prescriptive regulation.

Gower concluded by returning to the metaphor of the Perfect Storm, observing that the contemporary geopolitical environment is unusually volatile and fraught with risks that may persist. Against this backdrop of wars, economic sanctions, tariffs, and cyber-attacks, the resilience and skill of financial institutions will be critical. Quoting Franklin D. Roosevelt, he noted, “A smooth sea never made a skilled sailor,” reinforcing the need for leaders to confront challenges directly with preparedness and agility.

The speech signals APRA’s clear commitment to advancing operational resilience in the Australian financial sector through measured regulation, close industry collaboration, and an emphasis on risk culture. This fortifies the system’s ability to continue serving the community effectively amid mounting external pressures. Financial entities must therefore elevate their resilience frameworks and strategic risk planning to navigate an increasingly turbulent environment successfully.
