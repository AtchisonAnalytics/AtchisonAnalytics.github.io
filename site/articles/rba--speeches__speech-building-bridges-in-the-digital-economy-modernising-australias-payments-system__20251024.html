Speech: Building Bridges in the Digital Economy: Modernising Australia's Payments System

The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) Governor Michele Bullock delivered a comprehensive address on 24 October 2025 at the Bradfield Oration, emphasising the critical need to modernise Australia’s payments system to sustain economic growth and maintain public trust. This modernisation is vital as the digital economy increasingly shapes how Australians live, work and transact daily.

Bullock began by honouring the legacy of Dr John Bradfield, whose visionary Sydney Harbour Bridge symbolised transformative infrastructure connecting communities and fuelling development. She drew a parallel to digital infrastructure, highlighting that Australia’s payments system is now a foundational utility akin to physical infrastructure, underpinning economic activity and requiring continual innovation and resilience.

Australians now average about 700 digital payments annually, a sevenfold increase since 2000, reflecting the growing reliance on cards and bank transfers. Users expect these services to be safe, reliable, low-cost and easy to use. According to Bullock, Australia’s digital payments system generally meets these expectations and is often lauded by foreign visitors. However, she identified several opportunities for improvement and innovation.

The RBA’s Payments System Board regulates the payments system, balancing safety, efficiency and competition in the public interest. One example of this balancing act is the Board’s ongoing Review of Merchant Card Payment Costs and Surcharging, which is seeking regulatory changes to enhance fairness while accommodating providers' and users’ diverse perspectives.

Innovation is central to maintaining a fit-for-purpose digital payments system that adapts to evolving needs. Bullock cited Australia’s early adoption of contactless ‘tap-and-go’ card payments as a global leader in payment ease and convenience. This advancement required coordinated investment across banks, terminal providers and merchants, illustrating how scale and industry cooperation are essential.

Another significant innovation is the New Payments Platform (NPP), launched in 2018, enabling real-time, 24/7 transfers using PayIDs to simplify account addressing. While initial adoption was slower than anticipated, partly due to some banks’ delayed rollout of key services, the RBA intervened by urging compliance. NPP usage now averages 5 million daily transactions, underscoring the success of collaborative industry-government efforts in infrastructure development.

Bullock noted that such coordination can be challenging because individual payment providers often lack incentives to improve shared infrastructure. The RBA acts as a catalyst for collaboration, facilitating consensus rather than imposing regulation, seen clearly in the decade-old Strategic Review of Innovation that paved the way for the NPP by identifying gaps like real-time processing and richer payment data.

Looking forward, Bullock outlined four priority areas requiring further innovation and cooperation. The first is modernising Australia’s ageing Bulk Electronic Clearing System (BECS), which processes bulk payments such as payroll and government transfers. Currently reliable but lacking modern features like real-time 24/7 processing, BECS is slated for decommissioning by 2030. A partnership between the RBA, Treasury and industry has formed an A2A Payments Roundtable to create a public vision and transition plan for future account-to-account payments by mid-2026. Achieving a seamless shift will entail managing trade-offs among cost, reliability, innovation and stakeholder interests.

The second focus is enhancing cross-border payments. Despite domestic advancements, international payments remain costly and slow. The RBA participates in global efforts to deploy modern technologies in cross-border infrastructure, exemplified by the recent International Payments Service (IPS) on the NPP, which allows real-time, round-the-clock receipt of overseas funds. Addressing financial crime risks that otherwise increase processing delays and costs, the RBA has collaborated on Project Mandala with international partners to explore digital tools automating compliance and reducing failed transactions, aiming to make cross-border payments faster and cheaper.

Third, the payments system must better combat fraud and scams, which inflict serious harm on individuals and organisations. The innovative ‘Confirmation of Payee’ service rolling out on the NPP allows payers to verify account names before transferring funds, addressing a major source of scams. Banks also employ artificial intelligence techniques to detect suspicious transactions. Bullock warned that as fraudsters evolve, continuous innovation and cooperation will be essential. She highlighted the impending threat quantum computing poses to current encryption standards and the necessity of migrating card payments to the quantum-secure Advanced Encryption Standard (AES).

Fourth, operational resilience demands increased emphasis given rising cybersecurity threats, technological complexity and reliance on third-party providers. The RBA oversees governance and risk management of critical payments infrastructure, including legacy system vulnerabilities. It conducts research on systemic risks such as interdependencies that could amplify outages and promotes industry preparedness. Resilience measures encompass prevention, detection and mitigation of operational risks, ensuring minimal disruption if incidents occur.

Beyond policy and oversight, the RBA operates Australia’s interbank settlement infrastructure through the Reserve Bank Information and Transfer System (RITS), which processes about $300 billion daily equivalent to turning over Australia’s GDP every nine days. RITS enables secure, efficient real-time gross settlement of high-value payments and supports the Fast Settlement Service (FSS) for continuous 24/7 NPP transactions mostly settled in under a second. Following a significant system outage in October 2022, the RBA commissioned an external review and launched initiatives to enhance RITS’s operating model, IT controls and governance to meet extremely high availability and resilience standards.

Planning for the future, the RBA intends to explore modernising RITS starting 2026. Potential improvements include leveraging modern data exchange methods for better user experience, extending operating hours, and broadening central bank money settlement to other transaction types. Concurrently, the RBA is researching applications of digital forms of central bank and private money for wholesale payments and settlement through Project Acacia, collaborating with the Digital Finance Cooperative Research Centre and industry partners. This research provides valuable insights for evolving Australia’s settlement infrastructure amid rapid innovation.

While digital payments dominate, Bullock stressed cash’s ongoing importance for resilience, inclusiveness and storing value. Approximately 1.5 million Australians rely on cash for daily transactions, particularly among older and vulnerable communities. Cash remains a critical backup during digital outages or natural disasters, such as Queensland floods earlier this year. The RBA and government are committed to maintaining viable access to cash and improving the efficiency, sustainability and resilience of the underlying distribution system.

The RBA issues Australian banknotes, chiefly from its site in Victoria, supplying major banks for broader distribution. Australia pioneered polymer banknotes in the 1980s, enhancing durability and security through innovations like clear windows with holograms. These features have produced long circulation lives and savings exceeding $1 billion, alongside effective counterfeiting reduction. Security upgrades between 2016 and 2020 further lowered counterfeiting rates significantly; in 2025 one would handle over 165,000 banknotes on average before encountering a counterfeit, compared with about 32,000 in 2015. Nevertheless, the cash distribution system faces pressures due to declining usage, prompting consultations on regulatory reforms and strengthened risk management.

Concluding, Bullock invoked Bradfield’s visionary infrastructure projects to illustrate that building transformative connections often requires enduring commitment beyond immediate results. She urged all stakeholders to collaborate in fostering a payments system that remains safe, reliable, affordable and easy to use, while embracing innovation and resilience. This dual focus is crucial not only to meet Australians’ expectations but to unlock sustained economic potential. Maintaining and evolving the payments system is no longer optional but an imperative for securing Australia’s economic future in an increasingly digital world.
