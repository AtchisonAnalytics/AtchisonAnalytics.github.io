APRA Executive Director of Cross-industry Risk Chris Gower – speech to RMA CRO Conference 2025

The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) outlined its strategic focus for financial system stability amid growing geopolitical and technological risks in a speech on 4 September 2025. Executive Director of Cross-industry Risk Chris Gower emphasised the balance between safeguarding resilience and enhancing regulatory efficiency to support competition and innovation.

Chris Gower described APRA’s core mission as protecting Australians’ bank deposits, superannuation savings, and insurance claims payments while maintaining systemic stability essential for economic growth. APRA’s regulatory approach evolves with the operating environment, as detailed in its 2025–26 Corporate Plan, which addresses emerging risks and aims to minimise regulatory burdens without compromising prudential standards.

Globally, ongoing geopolitical tensions—including conflicts, power rivalries, and shifting trade policies—pose significant threats to Australia’s open, highly interconnected financial system. For instance, Russia’s invasion of Ukraine increased commodity prices and inflation, prompting rapid interest rate hikes. Financial institutions also faced urgent demands to implement sanctions. Gower highlighted that such unrest correlates with rising cyber-attacks, noting warnings from the Australian Signals Directorate and the Australian Security Intelligence Organisation regarding “relentless” cyber espionage by foreign actors that risks substantial economic harm.

Technological developments amplify cyber risks, with adversaries leveraging artificial intelligence to execute sophisticated attacks, such as deepfake voice and video fraud. Australia’s financial sector faces heightened vulnerability due to its growing dependence on digital platforms and third-party service providers. Gower cited the 2023 Qantas data breach as a cautionary example of how attacks on service providers can cascade across industries. A ransomware attack on a US cloud IT provider disrupted 60 credit unions, illustrating the systemic threat posed by service provider concentration.

Domestically, while global risks loom, Australia’s economic growth is forecast to improve modestly over the next year. Low unemployment and easing inflation, coupled with falling interest rates, should ease mortgage repayments and expand borrowing capacity. However, housing market risks remain prominent. Banks have more exposure to residential mortgages than comparators internationally. Housing supply constraints and rising prices, alongside persistent high household debt-to-income ratios, present vulnerabilities to financial stability.

APRA organised its Corporate Plan around four pillars: maintaining financial and operational resilience; responding to significant and emerging risks; achieving the right regulatory balance; and improving organisational effectiveness. The authority prioritises ensuring that banks, insurers and super funds possess the financial strength and operational robustness to provide essential services through all reasonable circumstances. This, Gower underscored, is the foundation of trust that has underpinned Australia’s 29 years of uninterrupted economic growth, surviving major crises like the Asian Financial Crisis and the Global Financial Crisis (GFC).

International credit rating agencies acknowledge Australia’s prudential framework as among the strongest globally, comparable to Singapore, Hong Kong and Canada. APRA’s ongoing supervisory focus includes revising the bank capital framework to phase out Additional Tier 1 capital instruments and consulting on liquidity framework updates to strengthen banks’ resilience to future crises.

Operational risk management receives growing attention, reflecting increased sector interconnectivity and reliance on technology. APRA introduced its first prudential standard on operational risk management in July 2025. In the coming year, it will rigorously assess compliance, emphasising institutions’ preparedness to handle disruptions. Governance reforms also remain critical, as governance quality closely correlates with resilience during stress. APRA received unprecedented engagement during recent consultations, involving over 150 stakeholder organisations, and plans to update its approach imminently.

Cyber resilience is a key emerging risk. Despite progress since the 2019 introduction of the CPS 234 information security standard, APRA judges the pace insufficient given escalating threats. It has called on institutions to increase investments in cyber defences and authentication controls. Credential stuffing attacks exposed weaknesses in superannuation funds’ member data protections. APRA is scrutinising responses and enforcing standards to safeguard sensitive information and high-risk transactions.

Regarding artificial intelligence, APRA intends to enhance monitoring of its deployment, particularly in large institutions, without introducing new regulations. The existing prudential framework is deemed adequate but will be adapted as AI technologies evolve to ensure appropriate oversight and risk management.

At the systemic level, APRA collaborates with other regulators via the Council of Financial Regulators (CFR) to mitigate geopolitical risk. A dedicated workplan addresses “severe but plausible” scenarios involving financial market shocks, sanctions enforcement, operational disruptions and foreign interference. Incorporating such wider vulnerabilities into routine supervisory assessments marks a crucial step in risk management evolution.

APRA also launched its first systemic stress test examining financial stability risks from interconnections between banking and superannuation sectors. The exercise involved a scenario of financial market turmoil and operational challenges, assessing institutional responses and liquidity pressures. Initial findings are expected within the year as the test proceeds to a second phase, deepening understanding of intersectoral risk transmission.

A distinct feature of the 2025–26 Corporate Plan is the explicit strategic objective of “getting the balance right” between regulation and market efficiency. APRA has long pursued proportionality, tailoring requirements to entity size and systemic importance, and continually strives to reduce regulatory burden. Recent efforts include halving policy consultations, streamlining reporting and simplifying prudential standards. The elevation of regulatory cost management to a primary objective reflects growing national concern over productivity drag from excessive compliance demands.

Following a July letter from the Federal Treasurer requesting measurable burden reduction actions, APRA identified multiple initiatives. These targets include halving licence application times to support new entrants; clarifying supervisory expectations on risk-related capital requirements to enable reductions; introducing a third proportionality tier for banks; lowering capital demands for annuities; promoting affordable reinsurance for general insurers; and pruning outdated or duplicative governance rules. APRA is also engaging with Treasury and ASIC to address overlapping reporting obligations under APRA and the Financial Accountability Regime (FAR).

While none of these changes alone will solve Australia’s productivity challenges, collectively APRA believes they will enhance competition, efficiency and innovation without sacrificing financial stability. Gower cautioned that risk elimination is unrealistic and undesirable: banks must lend, super funds must invest and digital systems must operate despite inherent vulnerabilities. Prudence lies in understanding, anticipating and mitigating risks rather than attempting total avoidance.

This philosophy echoes lessons from the GFC, when inadequate regulation allowed speculative lending and housing market excesses to trigger systemic crisis. Strengthened prudential frameworks since then built resilience that continues to underpin confidence in Australia’s financial system. APRA remains vigilant to the evolving threat environment and committed to a regulatory balance that protects stability while enabling growth.

Gower’s address thus charts a path for Australia’s financial regulators amid complex global and domestic risks. By maintaining robust prudential standards, addressing cyber and geopolitical challenges, and actively reducing unnecessary regulatory burdens, APRA aims to foster a financial system that is simultaneously safe, resilient, competitive and efficient. This balanced approach will be critical to sustaining Australia’s economic prosperity and investor confidence in years ahead.
