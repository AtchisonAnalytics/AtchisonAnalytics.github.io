Deputy Chair Margaret Cole - Remarks to the FSC Innovation in Retirement conference

APRA Deputy Chair Margaret Cole outlined the regulatory authority’s priorities for superannuation as the industry shifts into the retirement phase, emphasising the need for trustees to adapt to demographic and systemic changes. Her speech, delivered at the FSC Innovation in Retirement conference on 16 October 2025, highlights the critical challenges facing Australia’s superannuation system and the regulatory responses designed to safeguard member outcomes.

Cole identified three major forces driving change in superannuation. First, demographic shifts are pushing more Australians from accumulation into retirement. Treasury estimates some 2.5 million members will enter the retirement phase this decade, requiring trustees to develop strategies that adequately support these retirees. The second force is the rapid growth of superannuation assets, which now total $4.3 trillion, with $3 trillion managed by APRA-regulated funds. This makes Australia’s super system the world’s fourth largest pension fund, and by some projections, it could become the second largest by 2040. With larger asset pools, trustees face greater complexity in investment and risk management. The third force is superannuation’s rising importance to the broader financial system, where increasing interconnectedness with banking and other financial sectors poses potential systemic risks. APRA’s inaugural system-wide stress test on these risks is expected to be published later this year.

Cole’s remarks framed these contemporary issues within the historical context of Australia’s super system. She traced the origins back to the 1842 pension scheme established by the Australasian Bank—making a clear link between the past and today’s regulatory expectations. Unlike the original “we'll worry about it later” attitude towards pension funding liabilities, APRA insists on robust forward planning and rigorous risk management. This attitude reflects the agency’s commitment to the Retirement Income Covenant, introduced over three years ago, which mandates trustees to devise and implement retirement income strategies that support members as they retire.

Despite the Covenant’s intentions, Cole observed a mixed industry response. An APRA-ASIC thematic review a year after the Covenant’s introduction found a lack of urgency among trustees in meeting their obligations. More recent surveys, including a pulse check earlier this year, continue to reveal inconsistent progress, especially in measuring the success of retirement income strategies. Cole stressed that while some funds have embraced retirement as a strategic priority, many remain at baseline compliance without fully engaging with members’ evolving needs.

Understanding member needs and delivering tailored support has become central to regulatory focus. Trustees must help members maximise retirement income while managing risks and maintaining flexible access to funds. However, Cole noted many members over 65 still hold assets in accumulation-phase products, such as MySuper accounts, which may not be tax efficient. For instance, about $99 billion in assets remain in accounts held by members aged 65 and over, potentially costing members more in taxes than necessary. This underlines the need for clearer member communication and better-designed retirement products.

In response, APRA is facilitating greater product innovation, particularly by reducing capital requirements for life insurers offering annuities. The intent is to lower costs and encourage more widespread uptake of annuity products, which can provide steady income in retirement. APRA has consulted the life insurance sector and plans further consultations, aiming to finalise these reforms by mid-2026.

Cole warned of the substantial increase in assets moving into the retirement phase, projected to grow from $575 billion today to around $3 trillion in 20 years. This transfer substantially raises risks related to cyber security, operations, investment, and liquidity. She referenced recent credential stuffing cyberattacks targeting members, which exposed weaknesses in authentication processes and highlighted that retirees, who can access super funds freely, are increasingly vulnerable to fraud and scams. To combat these threats, APRA prioritises robust information controls, such as mandatory multi-factor authentication, and stresses the urgency of enhancing cyber protections especially given emerging quantum computing threats that could undermine existing encryption within five years.

Operational risk management remains critical amid technological growth and geopolitical uncertainty. Cole underscored the importance of meeting APRA’s CPS 230 standard, which governs operational resilience and material service provider oversight. Trustees must adequately manage these risks to maintain financial system stability.

The broadening investment horizon of super funds, notably greater exposure to offshore markets and private unlisted assets, also introduces new complexities. Overseas investments increase currency and geopolitical risks, while unlisted assets pose challenges for liquidity management and fair valuation. APRA is pushing for greater transparency to improve member outcomes through publishing fund-level data on retirement product returns and investment strategies. This will integrate into the Comprehensive Product Performance Package from the second half of 2025-26, enhancing visibility of product performance across the sector.

Complementing this, APRA collaborates with Treasury on a Retirement Reporting Framework set to begin in 2027. This framework aims to consistently monitor outcomes for retirees, enabling better policy and regulatory responses based on transparent data. The government’s confirmation of the framework’s scope is expected following consultations earlier this year.

Cole also introduced APRA’s new strategic objective of “Getting the Balance Right,” recognising the need to align financial safety and stability with competition and efficiency. Nine regulatory actions are underway to reduce unnecessary regulatory burdens. These include proportionality measures, removing duplicative rules, and improving data sharing across agencies, alongside the capital requirement reductions for annuity providers.

Australia’s superannuation system attracts international attention, especially due to its compulsory defined contribution structure. While some countries like the UK transition away from defined benefit models, Australia’s system benefits from a continuous influx of new capital through mandatory contributions. Cole’s recent interactions with UK regulators and international pension bodies illustrate this interest, which also reflects in the UK’s incorporation of Australia’s Value for Money framework within their own system design.

In closing, Cole described Australia’s superannuation system as globally admired but facing growing complexity that challenges members in retirement. She emphasised the necessity for trustees to address these challenges by improving retirement income outcomes, enhancing operational resilience, and understanding superannuation’s systemic importance beyond individual funds. The sector must be capable and well-prepared to manage the risks and seize the opportunities presented by three powerful forces shaping its future.

Her remarks serve as a clear call for trustees and the industry to adapt proactively, ensuring Australian retirees can rely on a robust, efficient, and responsive superannuation system in an uncertain and changing world.
