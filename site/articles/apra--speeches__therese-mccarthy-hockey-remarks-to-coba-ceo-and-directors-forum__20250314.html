Therese McCarthy Hockey remarks to COBA CEO and Directors Forum

Therese McCarthy of APRA addressed the COBA CEO and Directors Forum, highlighting weaknesses in banks’ crisis management plans that could threaten both institutions and their customers. She warned that many banks may be slow to detect problems and lack effective responses, risking serious financial disruption.

McCarthy pinpointed “triggers” — the mechanisms that identify when a bank is facing difficulty — as a key area needing improvement. Without clear triggers, banks risk discovering problems too late, reducing the time available to take action and increasing the potential impact. The other significant shortfall lay in “recovery and exit actions,” or the concrete steps banks plan to take once a problem arises. According to her remarks, several banks lack robust contingency plans that can be swiftly and effectively implemented in a crisis.

The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority functions as the primary regulator for Australia’s financial services industry. McCarthy’s comments come against this backdrop of APRA’s broad oversight responsibility. APRA supervises banks, mutuals, insurance companies, and superannuation funds holding an estimated $9.8 trillion in assets on behalf of depositors, policyholders, and fund members. Ensuring these institutions are prepared for stress events is central to APRA’s mandate to safeguard the financial system and protect consumers.

McCarthy’s observations imply that despite regulatory oversight, significant gaps remain in banks’ internal preparedness. The risk is that delayed recognition of problems and insufficiently tested recovery plans could lead to avoidable losses or instability. For institutions holding the collective wealth of millions of Australians, this shortfall compromises both their operational resilience and their ability to serve customers reliably during times of financial stress.

Improving triggers and recovery actions are essential steps for banks to meet APRA’s expectations and mitigate systemic risks. This requires greater attention to early warning indicators and the development of clear, practised protocols for intervention. If banks can address these areas effectively, they will enhance their capacity to protect themselves and their clients from the consequences of unforeseen shocks.
